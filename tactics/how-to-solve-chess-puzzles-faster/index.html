How to Solve Chess Puzzles Faster

Chess puzzles can be hard, especially to complete quickly. As a former tournament player, I have completed thousands of puzzles, and I know what works, and what doesn't. On this page, I help you solve puzzles faster with a method called checks, captures, and attacks, and I explain how to build pattern recognition. I also give a few concrete pointers at the end.

Improving Chess Editorial
Chess player (~2150 Elo on chess.com) · Experienced tournament player

1. Why Speed Matters in Chess

In a real chess game, you don't have all the time in the world. Your clock is ticking, and you can't spend 5 minutes in a position, only looking for tactics. You should be able to look at a position for 10-15 seconds, and evaluate if there's a possible tactic. Of course, sometimes the tactic takes some calculation, but you should see the possibility fast, and then decide if you need to spend further time calculating it. In my tournaments, I often had 1 minute left from the 90 minutes you have at the start. Even then, I often won games with tactics, which I had to spot really quick. Nowadays, I see a board, and can spot in 5 seconds if there are any major tactics, like pins or forks. This is essential to achieve a higher level in chess. That's why speed matters in online puzzles too. If you've ever solved puzzles on chess.com or lichess, you'll know your rating gain is higher the faster you solve.

2. Use the "Checks, Captures, Attacks" Method

I spot tactics fast using the checks, captures and attacks method. This consists of first looking at possible checks in the position, then possible captures and then possible attacks. These are all forcing moves; they force your opponent to respond to your move, not giving them time for their own plans. Let me give you an example below (It's white's move). This is quite a difficult puzzle, but ideal for my example. I am going to explain my thought process for this puzzle. First, I looked at possible checks, Nf6+ and Nc7+. After Nf6+, black can easily take it, so that one falls off. Nc7+ is nearly a fork, but black can take with the queen. This is something to keep in mind. Then I looked at possible captures. In this position, white can play Bxd6 and Nxe7. Both result in a simple capture without any problems for black. Lastly, I looked at attacks, and specifically, attacks to important pieces. I see four attacks to the queen that we need to review: Nxe7, Ne5, Nd4 and Bb5. Remember, we concluded that the queen was the reason Nc7+ didn't work. As we saw earlier, Nxe7 doesn't work, so that one falls off. Ne5 attacks the queen, but after dxe5 white didn't gain anything. Nd4 is interesting, because black can't capture it. However, black can safely move his queen to d7, and he is still guarding c7. Bb5 doesn't seem to work. Sure, it's a pin to the queen, but what if black just takes it? Remember Nc7+. That wasn't possible because of the queen, but after Bb5, Qxb5, white can play Nc7+, attacking the king, queen and rook. Black's king has to move, and white can play Nxc7, winning the queen.

Full continuation:

1. Bb5 Qxb5 2. Nc7+ Kd8 3. Nxb5

More on checks, captures and attacks

In this example, we saw how the thought process checks, captures and attacks helps us solve the puzzle quite quickly. Without it, who knows how long we would have been stuck looking for random moves. My friend, who isn't very good at chess but likes playing, sent a video of him playing with his dad, asking me for tips. My only tip was: Every move, look for checks, captures and attacks. You will be sure not to miss anything, and it is really fast to do. If you practise this for a while, you will eventually automate this, and spot a tactic within 5 seconds of looking at the board. How to Win at Chess by Levy Rozman is a great book for beginners, and spends 15 pages helping you understand the concept of checks, captures and attacks, and it is my favorite book for beginners. So, from now on, every time you solve puzzles, look for checks, captures and attacks, and you will be more accurate and faster in your puzzles.

3. Train Pattern Recognition

Checks, captures and attack is a system to tackle different positions, but what really trains speed is pattern recognition. After completing many fork puzzles, you will recognize a fork position. Same goes for different chess tactics, which you can learn about on my 7 Important Chess Tactics Explained. As an experienced tournament player, I recognize positions with a fork within a few seconds, which really helps speed up my puzzle solving. My coach once said that advanced chess players read positions in an instant. This means that we see a position, and instantly know if we can win a piece. I've made a short video of me playing puzzle rush to show how powerful pattern recognition is.

This kind of pattern recognition has come from playing thousands of games and solving thousands of puzzles. I would advise you to practise a lot; puzzles, games etc. A great book for training tactics is Chess: 5334 Problems and Combinations.

4. Common Mistakes That Slow You Down

Here are a few common mistakes that slow you down:

  • Guessing Guessing is the worst thing to do. First of all, you will fail almost every puzzle you try. Second of all, even if you're correct, you won't learn anything from it. If you have no idea what the answer is, instead of guessing, ask for a hint. That way, you get a little help, but you still learn.
  • Not looking systematically It is important to look systematically, because otherwise, you will miss obvious moves. Sure, sometimes it will be faster, but generally, looking at checks first, then captures and then attacks is the fastest and most reliable way to solve puzzles, especially if you have practiced it a long time.
  • Focusing on the wrong ideas In my experience, tactics are almost always focused on either checkmating, winning a piece or promoting a pawn. So if you see a move that maybe wins a pawn, look further before you play it, because often, there's something better. When I was a beginner, I made this mistake a lot, so I'm speaking from experience.

5. Practical Tips to Get Faster

  • Repetition Try to solve as many puzzles as you can. This will help with your pattern recognition, and your ability to implement checks, captures and attacks. When I was practising for the European Championship, I practised as many as I could with puzzles and chess games, which helped me a lot with my tactical vision.
  • Reviewing mistakes On chess.com, you can solve puzzles you had wrong, or you can filter on any motif that you find difficult, with the appropriate rating range for you. This is a huge recommendation, as you can personalize your training really easily.
  • Chess books I made a page that contains my personal best chess tactics books. You can find detailed reviews of each book, and at the end, you see which book fits best for you.

How to Practise

Build a daily routine. For example, set a goal to solve 10 puzzles using the checks, captures and attacks technique. This will help you train your desired pattern recognition, and your puzzle solving in general. You should also try 3 or 5 minute puzzle rush. This forces you to be quick, which also helps in games. And lastly, have fun. If you find normal puzzles boring, try puzzle rush survival mode, where every puzzle gets more difficult.
