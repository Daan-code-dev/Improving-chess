7 Important Chess Tactics Explained

Chess is all about tactics. As a former tournament player, I have come across so many of these. It is not only important to know how to use them, but just as important to know how to defend against them. This page will help you recognize these tactics in your game, making you able to capitalize on them and defend against them. This is crucial for chess improvement, especially for beginners and intermediate players. Read through it once, and you can always come back to check something.

1. Fork

A fork is when you attack 2 or multiple pieces at the same time with the same piece, so that you can capture one guaranteed piece. The knight fork is the most common one. In the diagram below, I put some demonstrative forks. In the top left, you see a knight which attacks the king, queen, and two rooks. Because none of the pieces can capture the knight, and the king is in check, the king has to move. After that, the knight has the choice between 2 rooks and a queen. Similarly, in the bottom right you see a pawn which is threatening two rooks. If one rook moves, the pawn can simply take the other rook. You can also often fork with the queen, because of its long range. Think of giving check, while at the same time, threatening a bishop. So, for future games, look out for moves where you can attack 2 pieces at the same time, and maybe you will be lucky!

2. Pin

A pin is a long range piece (queen, bishop, rook) that attacks another piece, which cannot move, because there is a more valuable piece or king behind it. To explain it better, look at the diagram below. The black rook is attacked by the white bishop. Your natural instinct would be to move the rook, but then the king would be exposed, making it illegal to play. Similarly, the black queen is attacked by the white rook, but if the queen moves, the king is under attack. It is a good habit that if you see for example a king and queen on the same line or diagonal, you immediately look for pins with your rook or bishop. And beware yourself, if your king and queen are on the same line or diagonal, make sure your opponent doesn't have a pin.

3. Skewer

A skewer is basically the opposite of a pin. A long range piece attacks 2 pieces along one line. The more valuable piece is in front, and when that piece moves, the piece behind it is captured. The diagram below shows two skewers. The black rook has to move, after which the black bishop is captured. Similarly, the black king needs to move, which results in the queen getting captured by the rook. You can spot these the same way you spot pins: Once there are two pieces on the same line, there may be a possibility for a skewer on the board.

4. Double Attack

Double attacks are incredibly powerful in chess. A fork is a form of a double attack, but a fork is so common that I thought it deserved its own section. A double attack can happen in a variety of ways: discovered double attacks, discovered checks, and attack-and-checkmate threats. I will explain each of them below, except for the fork:

Discovered double attack

Here you see a discovered double attack. The pawn moves to f5, attacking the rook, and at the same time, the bishop attacks the queen. This is quite difficult to find in a real game, but keep an eye on moves that attack a piece while freeing another piece.

Discovered check

A discovered check is part of a discovered double attack, but it's so powerful that I decided to explain it on its own. In the diagram below, the knight jumped from e5 to c6, with a check. Black has to deal with a check, and white can capture the queen. The reason this is so powerful is because black has to deal with the check. He can't play a sneaky in-between move, or start an attack of his own. To spot this in your games, keep an eye out for checks in general, as there are often only a few checks per move.

Attack and checkmate threat

An attack and checkmate threat kind of speaks for itself. You attack a piece, while creating a checkmate threat. Because the checkmate is more important, black will have to defend against that, leaving the piece undefended. In the diagram below, you can see that the queen is threatening to take the knight on c7, and is threatening to checkmate on h8. Black has to play f6, after which you can capture the knight. To see this in real games, look out for moves that threaten checkmate, and some of them will be an attack and checkmate threat.

5. Double Check

A double check is the most powerful move in chess. I have seen a lot of beautiful games ending with a double check. A double check is so strong because of the limited options for the opponent. Because you check from two sides, the opponent cannot block the check, which is often the preferred option. Black has to move the king, and then you can often take advantage of that, or it can be checkmate, as you can see in the diagram below. White moved his knight from b5 to d6, creating a double check with the queen and the knight. Black cannot block the check, and cannot move his king to another square. As a beginner, it is not as common as the other ones, but it's so beautiful and powerful that I thought you should know about it.

6. Deflection

Deflection is a bit more advanced, because you need to come up with two separate ideas. A deflection means to lure a piece away from defending something, and then taking advantage of it. In the diagram below, white plays Bd5, pinning the queen. Black can't just play king e7, as the bishop is indirectly eying the king. If black takes on d5, white plays Qxg7 checkmate. At first, that wasn't possible, because the queen was guarding that pawn, but by luring the queen away from the defence of that pawn, white was able to checkmate. In this case, you first had to see that if the queen wasn't on f7, white could play Qxg7. Then, you had to come up with the idea to lure the queen away with Bd5, making it quite advanced. It is surprisingly common, especially in high-beginner to advanced games. I come across this tactic quite a lot, so some people even trick me and use this tactic. This is why it's important to look for possible checkmating ideas, because sometimes you can create it, or defend against this kind of tactic.

7. Decoy

A decoy has quite some similarities with a deflection. A decoy, however, is used to lure a piece to a certain square, after which you can win the piece, or checkmate. In the diagram, you may think about playing Qh7+ directly. However, then black plays Kf8 and it is unclear how to move on. Instead, you can play Rh8+ first. This forces the king to take on h8, and then, Qh7 is checkmate. Just like deflections, decoys are hard to find, but not uncommon. Look for ideas, even if they don't work immediately. When the time comes right, you can strike with a decoy, winning a piece.
