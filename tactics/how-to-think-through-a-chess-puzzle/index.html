Checks, Captures and attacks

During my explanations of my thought process, I will often refer to a method called checks, captures and attacks. It basically means to tackle a position by focussing on forcing moves. You first look for checks, which are the most forcing. If that doesn't give an answer, you start looking for captures. If that doesn't work, you look for possible attacks. This method makes sure that you don't miss anything crucial, and gives a clear system for players. After a while of practising this, you will become more accurate and faster, because you don't miss simple answers.

400 Elo

This is a ~400 rated puzzle on chess.com. It's white's move. Try to solve it yourself first, and after that, you can check out the solution below.

My thought process

A lot of people might be tempted to take the black rook on f5 with the bishop. I mean, it's a free rook right? That is a mistake a lot of beginners make. Think about what I said earlier: checks, captures and attacks. This is the order you should use to solve every puzzle. I started with looking at checks. I quickly found Rxd8+. Then, I looked at what black can do. His only move is Rf8, after which I can just take on f8, and then it's mate. Taking on f5 doesn't even have to be considered when you know that Rxd8+ ends with checkmate. Bxf5 is a common mistake beginners make, because they see a free piece, and don't look further if there's anything better available.

Simplified Solution

1.Rxd8+ Rf8 2.Rxf8#. Bxf5 fails due to Nxd1

800 Elo

This is a ~800 rated puzzle on chess.com. It's white's move. Try to solve it for yourself first, and after that, you can check out the solution.

My thought process

Checks, Captures and attacks. That's the first thing I looked at. White only has one check, Qf7+. Because white's rook defends the queen, black has to move the king to h8. Here comes the tricky part. After Kh8, I looked again for checks, captures and attacks, and I saw Qf8+. After black plays Rxf8, white can play Rxf8 and it's checkmate. A lot of players would play Rxc7 instead of Rf8, seeing that it attacks 2 rooks at the same time. However, Black can play Qe8, defending both of the rooks.

Simplified Solution

1.Qxf7+ Kh8 2.Qf8+ Rxf8 3.Rxf8#

1200 Elo

This is a ~1200 rated puzzle on chess.com. It's blacks move. Try to solve it for yourself first, and after that, you can check out the solution.

My thought process

I first looked at any available checks. I saw two, Rxg2+ and Rh1+. After Rxg2+, white takes the rook with its king, and black has nothing. After Rh1+, black can play Rh8+, but white plays Kg1 and black didn't make any progress, except for losing his rook. Then I started looking at captures. I already dismissed Rxg2+, and Rxb6 does absolutely nothing, and white can choose which rook he takes. Then I looked for possible attacks, and this is a tricky one. I saw Rh8, threatening checkmate on h1. The only way white can prevent this is by moving his g pawn, moving his f pawn or playing Kf1. Take for example f3. I again started looking for checks. I immediately saw that Rh1+, even though it's not checkmate, still wins the rook on a1 after Kf2. Note that every other move to prevent checkmate is met with the same move. The hard part about this puzzle is finding Rh8, because it doesn't give a check or capture something.

Simplified Solution

1... Rh8 2.f3 Rh1+ 3.Kf2 Rxa1

1600 Elo

This is a ~1600 rated puzzle on chess.com. It's white's move. Try to solve it for yourself first, and after that, you can check out the solution.

My thought process

In this position, I immediately noticed Ne7+ as my first check. However, black can take with the knight, but I kept it in my mind. Rd6+ is another check, but that didn't seem to do anything. Then I started looking at captures; Nxh6 and Rxd5. Nxh6 is met with Rxh6, so that isn't an option. Rxd5 is met with black taking your rook. But wait, remember Ne7+? After 1.Rxd5 Rxd5, I saw that white could play Ne7+. Black has to move its king, and white can take back the rook on d5, ultimately winning a piece. This is known as a decoy. White lures blacks rook to d5, after which white can win it with a fork. A lot of players might see Rxd5, but think it does nothing after black takes it back. Another example of the power of looking at checks, captures and attacks.

Simplified Solution

1.Rxd5 Rxd5 2.Ne7+ Kd7 3.Nxd5

2000 Elo

This is a ~2000 rated puzzle on chess.com. It's blacks move. Try to solve it for yourself first, and after that, you can check out the solution.

My thought process

The obvious move to play is Bxd1. However, it's important to look at checks for your opponent as well. After seeing Bxd1, I looked for any checks black had. And indeed, after Bxd1, white can play Bxf7# checkmate. So, I needed to come up with another idea. I started again with checks, and quickly found Qd4+, threatening the knight, but is that all? After Kh1, black can take the queen on d1, because after Bxf7+, black can move his king to d8. This is the kind of puzzle most people fail. After finding Bxd1, people assume it's the answer and they don't look further. Try to standardize looking one move further, also in games, to avoid traps that your opponent set.

Simplified Solution

1...Qd4+ 2.Kh1 Bxd1 (Playing Bxd1 first doesn't work because white checkmates with Bxf7#. That's why black first plays Qd4+, to make room on d8 for the king.)
