7 Things I Wish I Knew When I Started Playing Tournaments

When I first started playing tournaments, I was quite unprepared, and that backfired, as you can read later on this page. Tournaments are way harder than playing online, especially mentally. Luckily, I've already made those mistakes so you don't have to. I will discuss 7 things I wish I knew before I started playing tournaments.

1. I Wish I Had Started Analyzing My Games Sooner

One of the most important aspects of chess is learning from your mistakes, especially during long games. I often didn't take analyzing seriously, and I did not do it regularly. However, at a certain moment, my coach suggested that I should use ChessBase, which is a site to analyze games and save the analysis. This helped me learn from my mistakes. And for beginners, I don't recommend ChessBase. It is quite expensive, and not needed for lower rated chess players. I do advise beginners to analyze their games, especially opening mistakes, as there is a relatively high chance this will come up in your games.

2. I Underestimated How Much Time Management Matters

Time management plays a huge role in tournaments. It doesn't matter if it's a blitz, rapid or classical tournament, each has its own characteristics. For blitz, it is important to move quickly. Focus on spotting tactics and attacking the opponent's king. For rapid, also keep your focus on tactics and attacking, but in the middle and endgame, make a plan so that you don't play random moves. As the famous Garry Kasparov once said: "a bad plan is better than no plan". For classical, it is important to force yourself to take the time. I personally went through a phase where I always had a lot of time left over. Funny enough, after that, I went through a phase where I always ran out of time. So, my solution is to set a target for how much time you want to have left by, for example, move 10, 15, or 20. I do have a funny story: one time, I was playing a stronger opponent, and I was running low on time, but I kept doing my best, and eventually, I won the game. After the game, he said to me: "You played better when you were low on time". That is probably due to pattern recognition. Intuitively, I played better than when I was thinking. This was, of course, quite unusual, but it does show how it's not the end of the world if you're low on time.

3. I Wish I Had Spent More Time on Openings

In tournaments, opening preparation is really important. I know what it feels like when you're worse after 8 moves and you still need to play 2 hours. It feels awful. That's why I decided to spend more time studying openings, and it really helped me. Also, learn what the important strategies and attacking plans are for your opening. This will save you a lot of thinking time during games, and it will help you win more games. I remember one time, during the European Championship, I was significantly worse after around 10 moves. I lost that game, and that was the trigger for me to study my openings, and it really helped me.

4. The Mental Side Was Harder Than I Expected

I think staying mentally cool is one of the hardest chess factors. It can be nerve-wracking to compete with opponents every time, having the desire to win and coping with losing multiple games in a row. There is a city close to me that I dislike, purely because I had a really bad tournament there, with four losses, one draw and one win. I think one of the most difficult things is showing up to a game where your opponent is higher-rated — around 100-200 points higher. You need to think: "I am going to win", not: "I hope I get a draw". It is important to trust your own abilities, even against higher rated opponents. This is especially the case with classical games, as you're going to play for up to four hours. But the most important tip: don't get taken aback by a loss or a bad tournament. At one of the next tournaments after my bad one, I received a rating prize, which means I had performed the best for my rating of everyone. Just keep playing, practising and learning, and eventually, things will go your way.

5. I Wish I Had Practised More Long Games

Before one of my first real tournaments, I had almost never played long games. This meant that I didn't know how much concentration I needed, and I got a bit overwhelmed. I recommend beginners to sometimes play a classical game on chess.com or lichess. It doesn't have to be often, once a week is more than enough, but get used to it. You can also practise your time management skills, and your ability to make plans. If you're playing a blitz or rapid tournament, I think the best way to prepare is just to play as many games as possible. These can be during your lunch break, for example, or on the train or bus.

6. I Wish I Had Enjoyed My First Tournaments More

The main reason for going to a tournament is to have fun, right? I often went with my close friend to tournaments, and it was ten times more fun. We could hang out during the breaks together, analyze our games and even play chess with each other during the breaks. Similarly, enjoy the atmosphere. You have so many people with the same interest as you, make new friends! Get to know people! I will take myself as an example. I once played an online tournament, and there I got to know a lot of people, including someone I played table tennis with. So of course, prepare for your next tournament and take it seriously, but remember to also enjoy it.

7. I Wish I Had Started Playing Tournaments as a Beginner

Even if you're still a beginner, it is never too early to practise. If you start early, you will learn how to deal with mental barriers and time management, making you more prepared for later. A specific example: once, in a really important tournament, I thought I was worse, and my opponent offered me a draw. I took it, but after the game, he told me that I could've won earlier. He then told me that when an opponent offers a draw, it is often because they see something that makes them worse. I didn't learn that, because I didn't play a lot of tournaments before that. Furthermore, playing tournaments offers a great way to properly analyze important games, which will help you improve a lot faster, especially because you will take tournament games really seriously. So, moral of the story: play tournaments as early as possible, even if you're a beginner.

How to Prepare for Your First Tournament

  • Play classical chess. Playing classical chess is a good way to get used to concentrating for a long time, and you will definitely improve.
  • Analyze your games. In preparation for your first tournament, analyzing your own games to know your weaknesses is a must, and then you can practise your weaknesses. (For example, you see that you misplayed a won endgame, and you can study how you can win it.)
  • Study openings. It's really important to be prepared for every move, e.g. e4, d4, c4, Nf3 etc. Especially for black, as you can often not choose the systems you're comfortable with.
