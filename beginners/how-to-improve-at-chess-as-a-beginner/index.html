How to Improve at Chess as a Beginner

You are a beginner and have no idea how to start or improve your chess skills. Believe me, I've been there. Through trial and error I've found out what actually works — and on this page, as a ~2000 rated tournament player, I will share that knowledge with you.

Improving Chess Editorial
Chess player (~2000 Elo) · Chess improvement guides

1. Learn the Rules Properly First

Think of chess like trying to speak a new language. You can't speak it unless you know the words. Before you start completing puzzles or analyzing your games, you need to know the rules of the game. This means not only piece movement, but also castling rules, checkmate rules, promoting pawns, and so on. A good book for this is Play Winning Chess, which also covers other important concepts in chess. Another way to learn the basic rules is through the lessons on chess.com and lichess.org — you can watch a video and practise what you learned with exercises. However, if you don't have a premium account, the number of lessons you can access is limited. Whichever way you choose, make sure to apply what you learn by playing real games online. That way, you can't play any illegal moves, which helps you internalize the rules naturally.

2. Focus on Tactics Before Anything Else

Almost all chess games are won with tactics, especially at the beginner level. Tactics are direct, concrete moves to win a piece or checkmate. This can come in many forms, such as pins, forks, skewers and many more. No matter how well you understand strategy or positional play, if you miss tactics, you will lose games. As a 2000 rated player, I have completed countless puzzles, all of which helped me improve. I would advise you to use books for learning new concepts and specifically training certain ideas. On this page, you can find my favorite books for tactics. You can complement this with online puzzles of all kinds of themes.

3. Learn Basic Checkmate Patterns

The essence of the game is of course to checkmate your opponent. The most important one is back-rank checkmate. Bobby Fischer Teaches Chess is an amazing book to practise all kinds of checkmates, with a heavy focus on back-rank checkmate. It is also important to know how to defend against these kinds of checkmating ideas. Just imagine the feeling when you've played a great game, have a complete winning position, but then blunder it all to an easy-to-prevent checkmate. However, practise is key. This book has 306 mates in one, 3400 mates in two, and 744 mates in three. It is also important to know how to mate with a queen and king vs a king, and with a rook and king vs king, as this is a common motive in chess, and will definitely win you many games. Keep in mind: No matter how good you are, checkmating ultimately wins you the game.

4. Play as Many Games as You Can

Practise is key. You can learn all the theory you want, but if you don't play games, you will improve way slower. Between over the board chess and online, I don't really have a recommendation. The advantage of playing over the board is that it's often more fun, because you can play with friends and have fun. However, playing online is ideal for regular days, where you may not have the time to go somewhere and play for an hour. I personally play most of my games online, but I find that over the board chess is just more fun. Then there is the time control. I would advise beginners to play mostly rapid chess. This is the ideal place between fun and learning. For improving in chess, it is far more important to think about moves that you play, rather than playing ten 1-minute chess games. However, if you find blitz way nicer, just play it. After all, chess is all about having fun.

5. Analyse Your Games

What's the point of taking a practise test without checking your answers? The best way to improve at chess is analyzing your games. Chessgoals.com recommends spending 20% of your playing time as a beginner analyzing your games, because you can review your mistakes so that they don't happen again. Try to prioritize longer games. It is far more valuable to analyze a rapid game than a bullet, and don't only analyze winning games. This is a common mistake by beginners: Analyzing games where you lost is more important than analyzing games where you won. You can use an engine to analyze your games: Chess.com and Lichess are both fine. I prefer Chess.com, because it is a little more user-friendly, especially for beginners. However, without a premium account, you can only analyze one game per day. When I am analyzing my game, I always write down one key thing that I could improve later, and I write that down in a notebook. That way you remember it, making you a better chess player.

6. Study with the Right Books

Studying with the right books is really important. As a beginner, getting a good foundation is key to becoming an intermediate player. It is important to find a book that suits your level, otherwise it will be pretty useless. If you are interested in chess books, you can check my top 7 chess books for beginners, with detailed reviews. Lastly, if you're a beginner, focus on tactics first. That's where the magic happens. If you're approaching intermediate level, you can shift your focus to all kinds of books like openings, endgames, strategy etc.

7. Be Consistent — Even 15 Minutes a Day Helps

Concistency works better than playing chess for 3 hours one day, and then stopping for 2 weeks. Try to set a goal for yourself like 3 games and 15 puzzles. That way, you will stay motivated and you will keep going. Don't set rating goals, as this will only stress yourself. When I was a beginner and I was training for my first tournament, I created a training plan each day. That worked really well, and I advise everyone who is really serious about chess to do so. If you do it purely for fun, just play whenever you feel like it.

Where to Start

So, first learn the rules properly. Focus on tactics and mating patterns, combined with playing a lot of games. Make sure to analyze some of your games, preferably the longer ones. Study with the right books, and be consistent. But keep in mind, having fun is the primary goal!
